# Scientific Writing Principles

Use this reference when diagnosing prose, performing a substantial revision, or explaining why a change improves readability. Apply the checks selectively; they are heuristics, not an algorithm or a universal style sheet.

## Reader-expectation diagnostics

These checks synthesize Gopen and Swan's reader-expectation approach:

| Diagnostic | Question to ask | Typical repair |
|---|---|---|
| Subject–verb continuity | Does substantial material interrupt the grammatical subject and its verb? | Move secondary detail, split the sentence, or recast it so the core action arrives sooner. |
| Topic position | Can the reader tell whose or what story the sentence continues? | Begin with the continuing topic, prior information, or the context needed to interpret what follows. |
| Stress position | Does the sentence end on the information meant to receive emphasis? | Move the consequential new information toward syntactic closure. |
| Old-to-new flow | Does each sentence connect backward before advancing the argument? | Make the logical bridge explicit; do not require the reader to infer a missing link. |
| Action in the verb | Are the important actions hidden in abstract nouns while weak verbs carry the sentence? | Restore the scientific action to a precise verb when that clarifies agency and logic. |
| One unit, one job | Is a sentence or paragraph competing to make several unrelated points? | Separate functions or make their relationship explicit. |
| Structural emphasis | Do position and structure emphasize what is scientifically important? | Align headings, paragraph endings, sentence endings, figures, and tables with the intended hierarchy. |

Passive voice is not inherently unclear. Choose active or passive constructions according to the continuing topic, relevant agency, and intended emphasis. Likewise, sentence length alone is a poor diagnostic: difficulty often comes from unresolved syntax, too many competing emphases, or missing logical connections.

### Compact example

Before: "An evaluation of the intervention was conducted, and a reduction in error was observed in the high-noise condition."

After: "We evaluated the intervention in the high-noise condition, where it reduced error."

The revision names the action in verbs, makes agency explicit, and ends on the result. If the actor is irrelevant or unknown, preserve that fact rather than adding "we."

## Paper- and paragraph-level checks

- State what problem the work addresses, what is not yet known or solved, what this work contributes, and what evidence supports each contribution.
- Give each section a clear role in the paper's larger argument. Keep methods, results, and interpretation distinct enough that readers can tell what was done, what was observed, and what the authors infer.
- Open each paragraph with enough context to orient the reader; develop one main point; close with the consequence, evidence, or transition that deserves emphasis.
- Prefer one recurring example or use case when it clarifies multiple stages of an argument. Switching examples repeatedly increases the reader's context burden.
- Make figure and table captions sufficiently self-contained to identify what is shown, what is compared, and how essential notation or uncertainty should be read.

## Precision and consistency

- Use one term for one stable concept. Synonyms can create the false impression that multiple constructs exist.
- Define abbreviations before use when readers need them, and consider whether independently read sections such as the abstract require fewer abbreviations.
- Replace vague objects such as "this," "it," or "the approach" when more than one antecedent is plausible.
- Prefer specific scientific actions and objects over metadiscourse such as "this study aims to investigate."
- Use connective words only when they state the actual relationship: cause, contrast, qualification, sequence, or consequence.

## Evidence and citations

- Place a citation where its scope is unambiguous and ensure it supports the exact claim made.
- Center the scientific proposition unless the identity or disagreement of authors is itself relevant.
- Prefer a peer-reviewed version over a preprint when they represent the same work and the published version is authoritative for the claim.
- Use page numbers for direct quotations or when required by the relevant style guide.
- Treat source selection, publisher preferences, citation count, and citation syntax as field- and venue-dependent. Do not turn a collaborator's local convention into a universal rule.

## Revision passes

1. **Argument pass:** map claims to evidence; flag gaps, overreach, and inconsistent scope.
2. **Structure pass:** test the job and ordering of sections and paragraphs.
3. **Continuity pass:** inspect topic progression, old-to-new flow, and missing logical bridges.
4. **Sentence pass:** inspect subjects, verbs, actions, stress positions, ambiguity, and unnecessary complexity.
5. **Consistency pass:** normalize terminology, abbreviations, notation, tense choices, cross-references, and citation placement according to project or venue rules.
6. **Cold-reading pass:** review paragraph by paragraph in a changed order or after a break when practical; this reduces dependence on remembered context.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better response |
|---|---|
| Polishing fluent prose before checking the argument | Diagnose the question–claim–evidence chain first. |
| Shortening every sentence mechanically | Repair information placement and logical continuity; split only where doing so helps. |
| Treating active voice as mandatory | Choose the construction that maintains the intended topic and agency. |
| Improving rhetoric by strengthening the claim | Preserve evidential scope and report the support gap. |
| Applying personal LaTeX, language, or citation preferences universally | Defer to the manuscript, authors, discipline, and target venue. |
| Rewriting beyond the requested scope | Make the smallest coherent improvement and identify adjacent issues separately. |

## Sources

- George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, [The Science of Scientific Writing](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf), *American Scientist* 78 (1990), 550–558. The reader-expectation diagnostics above paraphrase its principles of topic position, stress position, subject–verb continuity, context before new information, action in verbs, and alignment between structural and substantive emphasis.
- Nils Kuehl, [Some rules to follow when writing a paper](https://github.com/nkukit/howtopaper/blob/master/README.md). The practical checks above adapt its advice on reader guidance, coherent section flow, recurring examples, terminology and abbreviation consistency, citation placement, self-contained captions, and multi-pass editing. Its author- or venue-specific preferences are intentionally not universalized.
